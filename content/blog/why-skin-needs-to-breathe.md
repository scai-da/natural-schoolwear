Skin doesn't breathe in the way lungs do. It doesn't take in oxygen to power the body. So when we say skin needs to "breathe", what we really mean is that it needs to **release heat and moisture freely**. And that's one of its most important jobs.

### Skin, the body's cooling system

Your child's skin is their largest organ. Alongside keeping germs out and moisture in, it's the main way the body controls its temperature. It does this in two ways:

1. **Moving heat to the surface.** When the body warms up, blood vessels near the skin widen, carrying heat out from the core so it can escape into the air. Children rely heavily on this: research shows they direct more blood flow to the skin than adults do.
2. **Sweating.** Sweat glands release water onto the skin. As that water evaporates, it takes heat with it. This is the body's most powerful cooling tool.

The key word is *evaporates*. Sweat only cools you when it turns to vapour and leaves the skin. Sweat that sits trapped against the skin, or soaks into a fabric that won't let it go, does much less cooling and much more irritating.

### Where clothing comes in

Clothing creates a thin layer of air between the fabric and the skin, sometimes called the clothing microclimate. When that microclimate stays cool and dry, children feel comfortable. When it becomes hot and humid, they feel sticky, itchy and irritable.

What decides the microclimate is how easily the fabric lets three things pass:

- **Air**, which carries heat away (air permeability)
- **Water vapour**, the invisible form of sweat (water vapour permeability)
- **Liquid sweat**, which some fibres absorb and spread so it can evaporate

A truly breathable fabric does all three well.

### What makes fabric breathable

Breathability depends on the fibre, the yarn, how tightly the fabric is woven or knitted, and any finishes applied.

Cotton is naturally hydrophilic, meaning it attracts water. It can absorb a meaningful amount of moisture and still feel dry to the touch, then release it to the air. Textile research comparing knitted fabrics has found 100% cotton generally gives better air and water vapour permeability than 100% polyester of a similar structure.

Polyester is hydrophobic: it absorbs very little moisture. Performance sportswear gets around this with special yarns and knit structures that move sweat along the fibres. But standard polyester and poly-cotton uniform fabrics usually aren't engineered that way.

Finishes matter too. Coatings that make fabric water- or stain-repellent can reduce how readily the fabric handles moisture.

### What happens when skin can't breathe

When heat and moisture build up against skin over a long day, the common results are:

- **Heat rash (miliaria)**, small itchy bumps from blocked sweat ducts
- **Chafing**, where damp skin rubs against fabric
- **Eczema flares**, as sweat and heat are well-known triggers
- **General discomfort**, which affects concentration and mood

None of these are dramatic. But for a child who's uncomfortable for hours every day, they add up.

### Choosing for breathability

- Choose natural fibres like cotton for items worn next to skin
- Avoid unnecessary repellent finishes
- Look for a slightly relaxed fit, which lets air move
- Pick lighter weights for summer terms

Natural Schoolwear uses organic cotton without repellent coatings, cut to let air circulate. It's a simple idea: let skin do the job it was designed to do.
