Few parents have time to iron five school shirts every Sunday night. So uniforms labelled "easy-care", "non-iron", "permanent press" or "crease-resistant" have understandably become the norm. They come out of the dryer looking presentable, and that's a genuine help.

But the technology behind that smooth finish has a long history with an ingredient most of us associate with science labs: formaldehyde.

### How wrinkle-free fabric is made

Cotton wrinkles because its fibres bend and hold new positions when they're damp and pressed. To stop that, manufacturers apply resins that cross-link the fibres, locking them into shape. Many of the most effective and affordable of these resins are formaldehyde-based, often called durable press resins or textile finish resins.

Once cured, most of the formaldehyde is bound into the fabric. But some can be released over time as "free" formaldehyde, especially when new garments meet heat and moisture. Think of a warm, sweaty child in a new shirt.

### Why it matters for skin

Formaldehyde is a well-known skin sensitiser. Dermatology literature has documented allergic contact dermatitis from formaldehyde textile resins for decades, and specialists consider it likely to be underdiagnosed. One reason is that patch-testing the fabric itself often gives a false negative, so the cause can be missed.

Reactions tend to appear where clothing presses and rubs against skin: the neck, underarms, waistband, inner thighs and the backs of knees. Heat, sweat, pressure and friction all make them more likely, which describes a typical school day rather well.

For most children, the small amounts in modern clothing won't cause a problem. For children who already have sensitive skin or eczema, it's one more potential irritant worth reducing.

### Are there limits?

Some countries set strict limits. Japan, for example, has long capped formaldehyde in textiles for young children at very low levels, and the OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 certification sets tight limits, with the strictest category reserved for baby items. The Global Organic Textile Standard (GOTS) goes further and prohibits formaldehyde in certified textiles altogether.

Australia does not have a specific mandatory limit for formaldehyde in clothing. That puts more of the responsibility on parents and brands.

### How to reduce exposure

- **Wash before first wear.** Washing new clothes can significantly reduce free formaldehyde. Make it a habit with every new uniform item.
- **Be cautious with "non-iron" claims.** Easy-care labels don't always mean formaldehyde resins, but they're a reason to ask.
- **Notice the pattern.** If your child develops itchy, red patches that match where a garment sits, talk to your GP or a dermatologist. Mention the clothing, and ask about patch testing for textile allergens.
- **Choose certified fabrics.** GOTS-certified garments can't use formaldehyde. OEKO-TEX certification limits it.
