Think about the clothes your child wears most. Not their favourite hoodie or their weekend sneakers, but the school uniform. Five days a week, around forty weeks a year, for thirteen years. By the time they finish Year 12, a school uniform has spent more hours against their skin than almost any other piece of clothing they'll ever own.

Yet most of us buy uniforms the same way: we check the size, check the price, and tick the box on the school's list. We rarely ask what the fabric is made of, how it was finished, or how it will feel at 2pm on a 34-degree day in February.

### More than a dress code

A uniform does a lot of work. It has to survive the playground, the canteen, the art room and the walk home. It has to hold up in the wash week after week. Manufacturers have responded to these demands with clever solutions: synthetic blends that resist creasing, finishes that repel stains, and dyes that keep colours bright through hundreds of washes.

Those solutions solve real problems for busy families. But some of them come with trade-offs that are only now becoming clear.

### What researchers have started to find

In 2022, a peer-reviewed study tested 72 children's textile products sold as stain-resistant in the US and Canada, mostly school uniforms. Every product contained PFAS, the so-called "forever chemicals" used to repel stains and water. School uniforms carried higher levels than bibs, hats and swimwear, and levels comparable to outdoor jackets.

That's one example. Others include formaldehyde-based resins used to make fabrics wrinkle-free, and certain synthetic dyes that dermatology research has linked to skin reactions in sensitive people. None of these is cause for panic. But together, they're a good reason to look a little closer at what our kids wear all day.

### Then there's the heat

Australian school days are long and often hot. Kids sit in classrooms, run around at lunch, play sport, and then sit some more. Fabrics that don't let air and moisture move freely can leave skin damp, warm and irritated by the end of the day. For the many Australian children who live with eczema, that discomfort can be more than a minor annoyance.
