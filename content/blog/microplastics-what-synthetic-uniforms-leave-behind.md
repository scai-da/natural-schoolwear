Most of the conversation about school uniforms focuses on what's good for the child wearing them. But uniforms also have a life beyond the wardrobe, and for synthetic fabrics, that includes the washing machine.

### Where microplastics come from

Microplastics are tiny plastic particles less than 5mm across. One of the biggest sources is synthetic clothing. When fabrics like polyester, nylon and acrylic are washed, they shed microscopic fibres. Those fibres are too small to be caught by most washing machine filters or fully captured by wastewater treatment, so many end up in rivers and oceans.

Research on textile washing has repeatedly confirmed that synthetic fabrics release microfibres in the wash, with the amount depending on the fabric construction, the wash temperature, the spin speed and the age of the garment.

### Why school uniforms matter

School uniforms are a big category of synthetic clothing in Australia. Think about it:

- Millions of Australian students wear a uniform
- Many uniform items are polyester or poly-cotton blends
- They're washed often, sometimes several times a week
- They're used for years and then often discarded

That adds up to a lot of washing, and a lot of shedding.

Microplastics have now been found in oceans, soil, drinking water, food and the human body. Scientists are still working to understand what that means for health, but reducing how much plastic we send down the drain is widely seen as worthwhile.

### What about natural fibres?

Cotton also sheds fibres in the wash. The difference is that cotton is a plant fibre made of cellulose, which can biodegrade under the right conditions. Untreated cotton fibres break down; polyester fibres essentially don't.

There's a nuance here too: heavily chemically finished cotton may break down less readily and can carry its finishes into the environment. That's another reason organic, minimally finished cotton is the cleaner choice.

### And at the end of a uniform's life

Polyester uniforms that end up in landfill can take decades or longer to break down, and they may shed microplastics as they do. Organic cotton uniforms without synthetic finishes are better suited to composting, recycling into new fibre, or donating to second-hand uniform shops.

### Simple ways to reduce shedding

Whatever uniforms your family uses, you can cut down on microfibre release:

1. **Wash full loads.** Less friction between garments means less shedding.
2. **Wash cold.** Lower temperatures are gentler on fibres.
3. **Use a shorter cycle and lower spin.** Less agitation, fewer fibres released.
4. **Air-dry instead of tumble-drying.** It's gentler on all fabrics and saves power.
5. **Try a microfibre-catching laundry bag or filter.** These capture some of the shed fibres.
6. **Buy fewer, better pieces.** Longer-lasting uniforms mean less replacement and less waste.

### Passing uniforms on

Most Australian schools run second-hand uniform shops. Passing on good-condition uniforms is one of the simplest ways to cut waste. Choosing durable, well-made items that last long enough to have a second or third owner makes a real difference.
