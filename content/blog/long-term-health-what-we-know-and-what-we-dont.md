When parents read about chemicals in clothing, the big question is usually: *could this affect my child's health in the future?* It's a fair question, and it deserves an honest answer, not a scary one.

So here's what the science can and can't tell us right now.

### What we know with reasonable confidence

**1. Skin sensitisation can be lifelong.** Once someone develops an allergy to a substance like formaldehyde or a specific textile dye, that sensitivity usually stays. Allergic contact dermatitis is well documented for both. Reducing early exposure to known sensitisers is a sensible way to lower the chance of developing an allergy in the first place.

**2. PFAS build up in the body.** PFAS are called forever chemicals because they break down very slowly. Some accumulate in the body over years. Research has associated certain PFAS with effects including changes to thyroid function, cholesterol, immune response (including reduced vaccine response in children), and some cancers.

**3. Children's exposure is proportionally higher.** Because children weigh less, the same exposure means a bigger dose per kilogram. Their bodies are also still developing. That's why health agencies consistently treat children as a priority group when assessing chemical risk.

**4. Eczema in childhood is common and can persist.** Australian research shows eczema affects a large share of young children, and for some it continues into adulthood. Managing triggers, including irritating fabrics, helps day-to-day comfort.

### What we don't know

**We don't know how much uniforms contribute to total PFAS levels.** PFAS reach us through food, drinking water, dust, packaging and many products. The North American uniform researchers estimated exposure through skin contact with uniforms, but they also stressed that uniforms are one factor among many.

**We don't know how much PFAS absorbs through skin in real-world wear.** Absorption through skin varies by chemical, and research on this is still developing.

**We can't link any specific illness to a specific uniform.** Long-term health effects come from the combined effect of many exposures over many years. No responsible scientist would say a school shirt causes a particular disease.

### Why act if the evidence isn't complete?

Because the precautionary approach makes sense when:

- the possible harm is serious and potentially long-lasting
- the exposure is avoidable
- the alternative is easy and has no downside

Stain-resistant and wrinkle-free finishes are conveniences, not necessities. Choosing uniforms without them doesn't cost your child anything except perhaps a few creases and an extra wash. That's a very low price for reducing an avoidable exposure.

It's the same thinking behind choosing BPA-free bottles or avoiding unnecessary fragrances on a baby's skin. Not fear, just sensible reduction where it's easy.

### Keep perspective

If your child has worn conventional uniforms for years, please don't panic. The most important things for long-term health are still the basics: good food, activity, sleep, sun protection and regular check-ups. Swapping to cleaner uniforms is a positive step, not an emergency.
