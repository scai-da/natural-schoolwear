When we think about chemicals in everyday products, we usually think about how much is in them. Scientists also think about something just as important: how long we're in contact with them, and how.

That's where school uniforms stand out.

### Counting the hours

Let's add up a typical school day for an Australian primary school student:

- Dressed by 7:30am
- Travel to school, then 6 to 6.5 hours of school
- Before or after school care for many families, often until 5 or 6pm
- Home, and often still in uniform until bath or dinner

It's easy to reach eight to ten hours. The researchers behind the North American uniform study made the same point: uniforms are worn directly on the skin for up to eight hours a day, and in many cases longer.

Across a school year of around 200 days, that's 1,600 hours or more. Across thirteen years of schooling, it's more than 20,000 hours.

### Close contact changes the picture

A rain jacket might contain similar levels of a chemical finish to a stain-resistant uniform. But you wear a rain jacket over other clothes, for short periods, when it's raining. A uniform is the layer touching bare skin: the polo collar against the neck, the waistband against the stomach, the dress or shorts against the thighs.

Skin is a good barrier, but it isn't a perfect one. Some substances can move from fabric onto skin and be absorbed, and that transfer tends to increase with:

- **Heat**, which warms the skin and the fabric
- **Sweat**, which can help draw substances out of textiles
- **Friction**, from running, sitting and moving
- **Time**, because exposure accumulates hour after hour

A school day has all four.

### Smaller bodies, bigger share

Children also weigh less than adults. When researchers calculate exposure, they usually express it per kilogram of body weight. The same amount of a chemical reaching a 25kg child's skin is a much bigger dose, relatively speaking, than for a 75kg adult. That's one reason the uniform researchers raised children's exposure as a particular concern.

### It's about reducing, not panicking

None of this means a single uniform will harm your child. Most exposures from clothing are small, and they're one part of a much bigger picture that includes food, water, dust and other products.

But wear time is the reason small choices add up. If you were going to choose one item in your child's wardrobe to make as clean and gentle as possible, it makes sense to start with the one they wear the longest.

### Simple steps

- **Change after school.** Getting into comfortable home clothes cuts a couple of hours of uniform time each day, and lets skin recover from the day's sweat.
- **Wash new items first.** It reduces loose dyes and finishing residues.
- **Prioritise next-to-skin items.** Shirts, polos, dresses and underlayers matter more than blazers worn over the top.
- **Choose natural, certified fabrics.** Organic cotton with GOTS certification avoids the most concerning finishes.
