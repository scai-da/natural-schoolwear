In most parts of the world, "keep cool" and "cover up" pull in opposite directions. In Australia, we need both. We have some of the highest UV levels on the planet, and childhood sun exposure is a known risk factor for skin cancer later in life. At the same time, kids need to stay comfortable through long, hot school days.

The good news: the right fabric and design can do both jobs.

### How much skin do uniforms cover?

Researchers at James Cook University assessed nearly 500 summer uniforms from 244 Queensland primary schools. They found that most uniforms covered around 62% of the body (excluding the head). Shirts covered roughly 30%, shorts and skirts around 20%, and socks and shoes about 12%.

Their conclusion was that many summer uniforms didn't give children enough protection from ultraviolet radiation. Short sleeves and short hemlines leave arms and legs exposed during recess, lunch and outdoor sport, often at the peak UV times of the day.

### The comfort trap

It's understandable why summer uniforms are skimpy: covering up in hot fabric is miserable. If a long-sleeved shirt traps heat and sweat, kids will roll the sleeves up, take it off, or simply be uncomfortable.

This is where breathability and sun protection meet. **Coverage only works if children are comfortable wearing it.** A breathable, lightweight fabric makes longer sleeves, collars and longer shorts realistic for a full day.

### Fabric and UV

Clothing protects skin by physically blocking UV. How well it does that depends on:

- **Weave or knit density.** The tighter the construction, the less UV passes through. Hold a fabric up to the light: the less you see through it, the better.
- **Colour.** Darker colours generally absorb more UV than pale ones.
- **Weight.** Heavier fabrics usually block more, but they're also warmer.
- **Wet or stretched fabric.** Both can reduce protection.

UV protection is measured as UPF (ultraviolet protection factor). In Australia, clothing sun protection claims are tested to the Australian/New Zealand standard, and UPF 50+ is the highest rating.

A well-made, tightly woven cotton can offer good UV protection without chemical UV treatments, while still letting moisture and air move. Getting that balance right is a design choice.

### Design features that help

- **Collars** that protect the back of the neck
- **Longer sleeves** or elbow-length sleeves
- **Longer shorts and skirts** that reach the knee
- **Lightweight, densely woven cotton** that stays cool
- **Loose fit**, so air flows and fabric isn't stretched thin

### The rest of the SunSmart picture

Clothing is just one part of being SunSmart. Australian schools also use broad-brimmed or legionnaire hats, shade, SPF 50+ sunscreen and sunglasses. Many schools follow a "no hat, play in the shade" rule.
