School colours are part of a school's identity. The bottle green blazer, the navy jumper, the maroon checked dress. But every one of those colours comes from a dye, and not all dyes are equal when they sit against skin for eight hours a day.

### The dyes behind synthetic fabrics

Polyester and other synthetic fibres don't take up dye the way cotton does. They're usually coloured with a group called **disperse dyes**, which are designed to penetrate water-repellent synthetic fibres. They're effective and affordable, which is why they're everywhere in sportswear, linings and polyester-blend uniforms.

The catch is that some disperse dyes are known skin sensitisers. In a large Italian multicentre study of textile contact dermatitis, dyes were responsible for nearly 80% of cases, with a handful of disperse dyes (Disperse Blue 124, Disperse Blue 106 and Disperse Yellow 3) the most frequent culprits. Half of the patients also reacted to other dyes or finishing resins at the same time.

Those blue dyes are often used to produce darker shades, which is worth knowing when so many school uniforms come in navy, black and dark green.

### What a reaction looks like

Textile dye allergy usually shows up as itchy, red, sometimes scaly patches where clothing fits closely and where sweat and friction are highest. Common spots include:

- Underarms and the sides of the chest
- The waistband and inner thighs
- The backs of the knees (think tights and leggings)
- The neck and collar line

The face and hands are usually spared. Because the rash often appears in skin folds, it can be mistaken for eczema, heat rash or a reaction to laundry detergent.

### Why it's hard to pin down

Dermatologists note that textile dye allergies are difficult to diagnose. Retail clothing rarely discloses which dyes were used, and the dyes in commercial products are often poorly characterised. Research analysing children's polyester clothing has found azobenzene disperse dyes, some of which raise both allergy and toxicity concerns.

So a child may react to one navy shirt and not another, with no way for a parent to tell why from the label.

### What about azo dyes?

Certain azo dyes can break down to release aromatic amines, some of which are classed as carcinogenic. The EU restricts these dyes in clothing. Certification schemes like GOTS ban azo dyes that release these amines, and OEKO-TEX tests for them.

### What parents can do

1. **Wash new uniforms before wearing.** This removes loose, unfixed dye and other residues.
2. **Watch for bleeding colour.** If dye stains the water or other clothes, some is coming off onto skin too.
3. **Favour natural fibres next to skin.** Cotton is dyed with different dye classes from polyester, so it avoids disperse dyes in most cases.
4. **Look for GOTS or OEKO-TEX labels.** Both restrict hazardous dyes; GOTS is stricter on what's allowed in processing.
5. **Keep a note.** If a rash appears, record which garments your child wore. It helps your GP or dermatologist.
